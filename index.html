December 15, 1969 — August 11, 2018

Terry A. Davis

He wrote an operating system by himself. Not a shell, not a toy kernel — the boot loaders, the compiler, the memory manager, the filesystem, the windowing system, the text-to-music editor, the flight simulator, and the games. Then he put all of it in the public domain and gave it away.

This page takes that work apart. Every number here was measured from the TempleOS source tree itself rather than repeated from a second-hand article, and the music in the Listen section is his own, replayed from the notation strings in his code.

The record

For scale: at the time of its release TempleOS was measured at roughly 121,000 lines. Windows 7 shipped at around 40 million. The gap is not a claim about quality — it is a reminder of what one person can hold in their head at once when nobody else is writing any of it.

Autopsy

The tree was cloned, every file walked, and every line classified as code, comment or blank. Two bundled data files — the King James Bible and a PCI device dump — are excluded from the totals and reported separately, because counting scripture as source code would be both silly and dishonest.

What "from scratch" meant here

Not a hyperbole. His own credits file states that TempleOS executes no code he did not write, aside from a handful of BIOS calls for configuration — and that he wrote the compiler too, so no GCC. That means the C preprocessor, lexer, parser, optimiser and code generator in Compiler/ are his, as is the #include-at-the- command-line workflow and the JIT that compiles a program the moment you run it.

Roughly 19,139 lines sit in Compiler/. That is a real compiler, written by one person, that compiles 50,000 lines in under a second.

The parts nobody expects

  • Boot loaders written from scratch, so no GRUB
  • RedSea filesystem, plus FAT32 for interoperability
  • VGA driver into 640×480 with 16 colours, text drawn by hand
  • PS/2 keyboard and mouse drivers
  • DolDoc — a markup, layout and rendering system for documents
  • Psalmody — a graphical sheet-music editor with playback
  • A JIT with no bytecode anywhere, plus an ahead-of-time mode
  • Self-hosting — it compiles a distribution of itself
  • 3D sprites as rotated meshes, not stored as GIFs
  • No networking, deliberately, on the grounds that it is not needed

Listen

TempleOS had no sound card. It drove the PC speaker — a single square-wave voice — through a notation string like 6hEqDC5B6CDhE. The parser below is a direct port of Play() from Adam/ASnd.HC, and the pitch comes from his own formula in Kernel/KMisc.HC: 440/32 × 2ona/12, where A4 is ona 60.

These are his compositions, lifted verbatim from the source tree — melody and lyric together, exactly as they appear in the files.

Glyphs

TempleOS runs at 640×480 in sixteen colours, which he attributed to a specific instruction. Those constraints are the whole aesthetic: a fixed sixteen-entry palette, an 80×30 grid, and an 8×8 bitmap font stretched twice as tall.

The palette, in order

Sixteen entries, indices 0–15, exactly as the kernel defines them. There is no anti-aliasing and no second shade of anything.

    Sources & method

    What was measured

    Every figure in the Autopsy section comes from cia-foundation/TempleOS, walked file by file at build time. Lines are split into code, comment and blank by a parser that understands both // and /* */. The commit counts come from git log on that repository. The songs are the literal Play() string literals found in Demo/Snd, Apps/Psalmody/Examples and Demo/AcctExample/TOS (including “Risen”). The GodSong panel reuses his duration/note tables with a browser PRNG standing in for compiled-only GodBits. The font is the sys_font_std[] table in Kernel/FontStd.HC, decoded pixel by pixel.

    Excluded from all totals: Misc/Bible.TXT (100,118 lines of King James Version, bundled for scripture search) and Misc/PCIDevices.DD (a 9,430-line device list). Including them would have added 109,548 phantom lines to the total — enough to make this page look more impressive than the work actually was.

    What is cited, not measured

    The biographical dates and the early life come from his Wikipedia entry and from accounts of his own writing — specifically ::/Doc/Credits.DD and ::/Doc/FAQ.DD in the source itself, where he describes writing the whole system “over the past 19.1 years (full-time)”.

    For the record: TempleOS is in the public domain, not merely open source. His own credits name the handful of things that were not his — a FreeDOS-derived font table, a Cyrillic font taken without permission from Orient Display, an LZW compressor adapted from a magazine article he had implemented years earlier at Ticketmaster, and a heap algorithm adapted from a VAX operating system. He listed all of it. That honesty is part of the work.
